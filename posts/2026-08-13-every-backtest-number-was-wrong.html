Every backtest number I had was wrong

I went looking for one suspicious result in a strategy tournament and found that the whole leaderboard was invalid — warm-up eaten out of the window, a helper quietly re-levering every portfolio, metrics that ranked a strategy which never lost money as the worst in the field, and three days of trading that happened at the weekend.

I have a research rig that races seventeen trading strategies against each other across four markets. It runs walk-forward backtests to rank them, then runs them all forward in parallel on a paper account to see whether the ranking survives contact with live data. Nothing about it trades real money, which is the only reason this story is comfortable to tell.

One strategy was scoring worse than it had any right to, and I opened the code to find out why. What I found was not a bug. It was six, and between them they meant every number the system had ever reported was invalid. Not slightly off — the rankings themselves were meaningless, because different bugs hit different strategies by different amounts.

This is the write-up. I have kept the emphasis on how each one hid, because that is the transferable part; the specific bugs are unremarkable.

1. The warm-up was eaten out of the window I asked for

Momentum indicators need history. A twelve-month momentum signal cannot produce a number until it has twelve months of bars behind it. So a backtest engine has to warm up before it starts trading, and the question is where those warm-up bars come from.

Mine took them from the wrong end. The runner filtered the price panel down to the requested start date and then skipped the first 273 rows of what remained. The effect: ask for three years, and the first third of it is a strategy sitting flat in cash, holding nothing, trading nothing — and every one of those flat days lands in the denominator of the Sharpe ratio.

On synthetic data where I could compute the truth, 34.9% of the requested window was dead, and the same strategy scored Sharpe 0.203 over the padded window against 0.572 over the segment it actually traded — a factor of 2.8, purely from dilution.

What makes this one nasty is that it is not random. A strategy that trades constantly gets diluted heavily; one that trades rarely barely notices. So the bug does not add noise to the leaderboard, it reorders it. The fix is that warm-up is drawn from bars before the requested window opens, and the window you asked for is the window you are scored on.

2. A helper that quietly re-levered every portfolio

This is the one I am least happy about, because it made an entire feature inert without ever failing.

There is a small function that takes a dictionary of target weights, caps any single position, and normalises. Its job is to keep a book from exceeding 100% invested. What it actually did was rescale before capping — which meant it also scaled up any book that summed to less than one.

Deliberate cash is not an accident. A strategy that finds three names worth holding out of eight slots is saying 37.5% deployed, the rest in cash. This helper heard that and returned 75%. A committee strategy where only some members voted got its partial position re-levered to full. And a volatility-targeting strategy — whose entire mechanism is scaling the whole book down when realised volatility is high — had its uniform scale factor divided straight back out on the next line.

The part that stings

That volatility-targeting strategy had a target-volatility parameter and a parameter grid that swept it. The parameter did nothing. Every point in that sweep produced an identical book — and the resulting duplicate rows then corrupted the rank-correlation statistic I was using to detect overfitting. A broken measurement, feeding a broken measurement of whether I was fooling myself.

The rule now is one sentence long and lives in the docstring: this function scales down only. A book that sums to less than one is a decision, not a rounding error.

3. The statistics that lied

A cluster of smaller bugs, each individually silly, all in the code that turns an equity curve into a number you rank on.

  • Standard deviation of a single observation is NaN. pandas uses ddof=1 by default, so Series([x]).std() divides by zero degrees of freedom. Any strategy with one return observation produced NaN Sharpe and NaN volatility, which then flowed into sort_values and into SQLite, where NaN sorts wherever it likes.
  • Sortino and Calmar returned zero for a strategy that never lost money. Both divide by a downside quantity — downside deviation, maximum drawdown. With no losing periods, that denominator is zero, and the guard returned 0.0. Zero is the worst possible score. A perfect run was being ranked last by two of the five metrics.
  • Annualised return used N periods for an N-point series. An N-point series has N−1 returns. A small error that grows as the window shrinks, which is exactly where you least want it.
  • Turnover measured intentions, not trades. It differenced target weights, but a trade band had already suppressed the small moves. It was reporting what the strategy wanted to do, and I was charging costs on what it did.
  • A missing price fabricated a round trip. A held name with a NaN close contributed zero to that day's equity and its real value the day after — a −100% move followed by a +100% move, in a position nobody had touched.

The general shape here is worth naming. Every one of these is a degenerate-input bug: one observation, no losses, a missing value, an empty set. Nobody writes a test for the case where the strategy did too well.

4. Three days of trading that happened at the weekend

The live paper race had no market-hours gate at all. I found this by reading the database rather than the code, which is the right order: of the three dates the race had ever recorded, one was a Saturday and one was a Sunday. Two thirds of the evidence I had about live performance was phantom bars, executed against Friday's stale closing prices, with modelled transaction costs faithfully charged on trades that could not exist.

Worse, all four markets were being stamped with a single New York date, so the Asian markets were being asked about a session that had not happened yet in one direction and had closed hours ago in the other.

The obvious fix is a market calendar. I did not want one — calendars need holiday tables, holiday tables go stale, and a stale holiday table fails silently in exactly the same way. What replaced it is a single data-driven rule:

A market's session is the last close date in its own freshly fetched price panel, and it steps forward only when that date advances.

Weekends skip themselves, because no new close appears. Holidays skip themselves, for the same reason. Crypto needs no special case, because it always has a new close. Each market gets its own local date for free. And a run against a stale cache is refused by the same check that handles everything else, because the panel's last date has not moved. No calendar table, nothing to rot.

It does impose one real constraint, which I wrote down rather than discovered later: the job must run after the close, or the panel does not yet contain the day it is meant to trade.

What I actually changed

The code fixes were the easy part. The habits that came out of it:

  • Probe the system, do not re-read the design. I found the weekend bug by querying which dates existed in the live table. I would not have found it by reading the runner, because the runner looks correct — the gate was missing, and missing things are invisible in code review.
  • Test on synthetic data where the answer is known. "34.9% of the window was dead" is a sentence I could only write because I built a panel whose correct result I could compute by hand. On real data it would have been a vague feeling that the numbers looked low.
  • Write the test that would have caught it, at the level it happened. The warm-up bug is now a test asserting the traded segment covers the full requested window. The normalisation bug is a test asserting a book summing to 0.4 comes back summing to 0.4. Neither is clever. Both are permanent.
  • Suspect the metric before the strategy. I opened this investigation because a strategy scored badly. The strategy was fine. Roughly half of what I found was in the scoring, not the trading — and scoring code gets far less scrutiny because it feels like plumbing.

The test count roughly doubled over the review, and all four tournaments were re-run on the fixed engine. I have not promoted a champion. Short-window live performance is mostly noise, and after this I am in no hurry to believe a leaderboard.

Why I am writing this down

My day job is machine learning on sequencing data, and the failure mode here is one I recognise from it exactly.

A backtest is a held-out evaluation. It is the thing that tells you whether the model works, and it is therefore the thing you are least equipped to check, because you have nothing better to check it against. When the evaluation is subtly wrong, every downstream decision is wrong in a way no amount of downstream care can recover — and it does not look wrong. It looks like a leaderboard.

In genomics the equivalents are familiar: a normalisation step applied before the split instead of after, a batch variable that correlates with the label, a metric that flatters the majority class. Same shape. The evaluation pipeline deserves more tests than the model, and it usually gets fewer.
