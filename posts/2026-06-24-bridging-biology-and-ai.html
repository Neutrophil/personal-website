Bridging the bench and the model: why I'm moving toward AI for Life Science

  • AI for Life Science
  • Genomics
  • Career

I spent the better part of a decade at the bench — pipetting, optimizing assays, chasing protein complexes through the nucleolus. These days I spend most of mine writing code: building whole-genome sequencing workflows and training models to tell cancer signal apart from noise. People sometimes ask whether that was a hard switch. The honest answer is that it felt less like a switch and more like following the same question into a new tool.

The data is never as clean as the slide deck

The single most useful thing the lab taught me is how a number actually comes to exist. Before a variant call is a row in a table, it was a fragment of cell-free DNA in a tube, a library prep that may or may not have worked, a flow cell with its own quirks. FFPE tissue deaminates. cfDNA is short and scarce. Every step adds a fingerprint to the data.

When you have stood at the bench, you read a model's errors differently. A false positive isn't just a misclassification — it might be a batch effect, a contamination artifact, a coverage dropout in a hard-to-map region. Knowing where the data comes from tells you which features to trust and which ones the model is quietly exploiting for the wrong reasons.

Why I'm optimistic

Genomics is, in a sense, an ideal substrate for machine learning: high-dimensional, structured, and increasingly abundant. Multi-omics integration — stitching together genomic, epigenomic and fragmentomic signal — is exactly the kind of problem where a well-posed model can see patterns a single assay cannot. We are moving from hand-crafted thresholds toward representations that the data itself suggests.

Why I'm cautious

And yet biology punishes overconfidence. A model that reports 99% accuracy on a clean cohort can fall apart on the next sequencing batch, the next hospital, the next sample type. In a clinical-diagnostics setting the cost of being wrong is not a worse metric — it is a patient. So I care as much about calibration, robustness and interpretability as I do about raw performance. The goal is not a model that wins a benchmark; it is one a clinician can trust on a Tuesday.

What I want to build next

I want to keep working where wet-lab reality and computation meet — models that are grounded in how the assay behaves, validated like a diagnostic and not just a leaderboard entry, and honest about their uncertainty. That is what AI for Life Science means to me: not applying a generic model to a biological dataset, but building systems that respect the biology and the messy, beautiful way its data is made.

This blog is where I'll think out loud about that — methods I'm reading, ideas I'm testing, and the occasional opinion. More soon.
