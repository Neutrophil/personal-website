Choosing ten stocks out of thirty with a QUBO

  • Optimization
  • Python
  • Quant

Suppose you have thirty candidate stocks and you want to hold ten of them. Not the ten with the highest expected return — that portfolio would be concentrated in whatever sector happened to run last quarter. You want ten that together give a good return for the risk they take on, which means the choice of any one name depends on all the others.

That dependency is the whole difficulty. There are C(30,10) ≈ 30,045,015 ways to pick ten from thirty. Evaluate a portfolio in a microsecond and you are still looking at half a minute for a problem that small, and the count explodes from there: fifty candidates gives you ten billion. Greedy selection is fast but wrong in a specific way — it will happily pick two names that are individually excellent and 0.9 correlated with each other.

One clean way to attack this is to stop treating it as a search over subsets and start treating it as minimising a quadratic function over binary variables. That object has a name — a QUBO, quadratic unconstrained binary optimisation — and a deep bench of solvers, including the physical annealers that gave the family its reputation. I do not have a quantum annealer. I do have numpy, and simulated annealing on a QUBO is a genuinely good classical solver.

Writing the objective

Let xᵢ ∈ {0,1} say whether stock i is held. Selected names are equal-weighted, so the weight of each is 1/K and any constant factor there can be absorbed into the risk coefficient. The thing to minimise is:

the objective
f(x) = − Σᵢ μᵢ xᵢ          expected return, negated because we minimise
       + γ Σᵢⱼ σᵢⱼ xᵢ xⱼ    risk: every selected pair's covariance
       + P (Σᵢ xᵢ − K)²     penalty: zero when exactly K are chosen

The first two terms are ordinary mean–variance. The third is the interesting one. A QUBO is unconstrained by definition, so "hold exactly ten" cannot be a constraint — it has to become part of the thing being minimised. Squaring the deviation does that: the term is zero when the count is right and grows quadratically in either direction, so an eleven-stock portfolio pays for its extra name and a nine-stock one pays for its missing slot.

Folding it into a matrix

To get a QUBO you need everything as xᵀQx. The trick that makes this work is that binary variables are idempotent: xᵢ² = xᵢ, because 0² = 0 and 1² = 1. That single identity collapses every squared term into a linear one, and the linear terms then live on the diagonal of Q.

Expanding the penalty with it:

expanding P(Σxᵢ − K)²
(Σᵢ xᵢ)²  =  Σᵢ xᵢ²  +  2 Σᵢ<ⱼ xᵢxⱼ
          =  Σᵢ xᵢ   +  2 Σᵢ<ⱼ xᵢxⱼ        ← because xᵢ² = xᵢ

P(Σxᵢ − K)²  =  P Σᵢ xᵢ (1 − 2K)  +  2P Σᵢ<ⱼ xᵢxⱼ  +  P K²
                └── diagonal ──┘     └── off-diagonal ──┘   └ constant ┘

The risk term splits the same way — its diagonal is the variances, its off-diagonal the covariances, doubled because an upper-triangular matrix counts each pair once. Collect the pieces and Q writes itself:

core/qubo.py
def build_q(mu, cov, k, gamma, penalty):
    n = len(mu)

    # Scale the penalty to the objective it has to compete with.
    obj_diag = -mu + gamma * np.diag(cov)
    scale = float(np.mean(np.abs(obj_diag))) or 1.0
    p = penalty * scale

    q = np.zeros((n, n))
    np.fill_diagonal(q, obj_diag + p * (1 - 2 * k))

    iu = np.triu_indices(n, k=1)
    q[iu] = 2 * gamma * cov[iu] + 2 * p

    # P·K² does not depend on x, so it cannot move the argmin. Keep it on the
    # side anyway, or reported energies will not match hand calculations.
    return q, p * k * k

The line that actually decides whether this works

Look again at those three lines computing p. They are the part I would have got wrong if I had written this from the textbook.

The penalty coefficient P has to be large enough that violating the cardinality constraint costs more than the return you gain by violating it, and small enough that it does not drown out the return-versus-risk trade-off you actually care about. Between those two failures there is a usable band — and where that band sits depends entirely on the units of μ and Σ.

Daily expected returns are numbers like 0.0004. Daily covariances are numbers like 0.0001. A penalty of P = 5 against coefficients of that size is not a nudge, it is a steamroller: the solver returns some arbitrary ten-stock portfolio, because getting the count right is worth ten thousand times more than getting the stocks right. Rescale your returns to annualised percentages and the same P = 5 becomes invisible, and the solver returns all thirty names.

The fix

Make P a multiplier on the objective's own magnitude, not an absolute number. Take the mean absolute value of the objective's diagonal — the scale at which this particular problem's return and risk terms actually operate — and set the penalty relative to it. Now P = 5 means "five times the typical coefficient", which is meaningful for any units.

This is the kind of thing that is obvious once stated and invisible beforehand. It is also almost undebuggable from the outside: a badly scaled penalty does not raise an error, it returns a plausible-looking portfolio that ignores half the objective. So the curriculum this lives in has a lab whose entire purpose is to let a reader set P by hand and watch both failure modes happen. I would rather someone break it deliberately than trust it silently.

To make that inspectable at all, the solver can decompose any candidate's energy into its three parts — return, risk, penalty — and the labs assert that the three sum to the total. If they ever stop summing, the expansion above has a sign error in it.

The annealer

Simulated annealing is a Metropolis random walk over bit strings with a temperature that falls over time. Early on it accepts moves that make things worse, which is how it escapes local minima; later it accepts almost nothing and settles. Written in terms of inverse temperature β = 1/T, a proposed flip with energy change ΔE is accepted with probability min(1, e^(−β·ΔE)), so raising β means "accept less".

The naive implementation recomputes xᵀQx for each candidate flip, which is O(N²) per proposal and therefore O(N³) per sweep. You do not need to. Split Q into its diagonal d and a symmetric off-diagonal matrix C, and maintain a field vector field = C·x alongside the state. Then flipping bit i changes the energy by a quantity you can read off in O(1), and repairing the field afterwards costs O(N):

core/annealer.py — the inner loop
field = c @ x                       # maintained incrementally from here on

for beta in np.geomspace(beta_start, beta_end, num_sweeps):
    for i in rng.permutation(n):
        v = x[i]
        s = 1 - 2 * v                          # +1 turning on, −1 turning off
        d_energy = s * (diag[i] + field[i])    # O(1), no matrix product

        if d_energy <= 0 or rng.random() < np.exp(-beta * d_energy):
            x[i] = 1 - v
            field += c[:, i] * s               # O(N) repair, not O(N²)

The schedule is geometric rather than linear — np.geomspace(0.1, 12.0, 1200) — because what matters is the ratio of β between consecutive sweeps, not the difference. Sixty independent restarts run from different random k-hot starting states, and the best of the sixty is returned. Starting from a state that already has roughly the right number of bits set gives the cardinality penalty a head start; starting from a uniform coin flip wastes the first few hundred sweeps just getting the count into range.

Making it reproducible, and proving it right

Two things I would not skip again.

Seed the restarts from a master generator, not a shared one. A single RNG shared across sixty reads makes results depend on scheduling order. One master default_rng(seed) that spawns a child generator per read gives you bit-identical output across runs, which is what makes a regression test possible at all. It also means the tracing that draws those energy curves has to be RNG-neutral — recording β, energy and acceptance rate each sweep must not draw a single number from the generator, or turning tracing on would silently change the answer. There is a test that runs the same seed with and without tracing and asserts the results are identical, because that is precisely the kind of thing that decays.

Race it against the truth while the truth is still computable. For N ≤ 16 you can enumerate all 2^N states — 65,536 of them — vectorise the energies, and find the exact global minimum. The tests do that and assert that annealing reaches it. Above sixteen the enumeration is hopeless, which is the point of the exercise, but by then you have evidence that the solver is not merely returning something plausible-looking.

Honest caveats

The expected-return vector is by far the noisiest input to any of this, and no amount of solver quality fixes a bad μ. The default candidate universe is today's large caps, which is survivorship bias baked in — so backtests over it are biased upward and are useful for relative ranking only. This is research and paper trading, not advice, and it does not trade real money.

What I would build next

The current formulation equal-weights whatever it selects, which is a real limitation. The standard extension is to give each asset several binary variables representing bits of its weight and add a budget constraint as another squared penalty — a binary expansion. Sector limits fall out the same way: one more squared term per sector. That is the pleasant property of this formulation. Every constraint you can write as "this sum should equal that" becomes another penalty and folds into the same matrix, and the solver never has to know it happened.

The part I find genuinely interesting is not the finance. It is that a discrete selection problem with interacting choices — pick a subset where the value of each element depends on the others — shows up everywhere. Feature selection under redundancy is the same shape. So is choosing a panel of markers where you want coverage without measuring the same biology four times. The matrix changes; the machinery does not.
